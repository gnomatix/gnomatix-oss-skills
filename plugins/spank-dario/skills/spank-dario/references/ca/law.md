## Clarification

- Moral rights "may not be assigned but may be waived in whole or in part" (s. 14.1(2)).
- An act or omission contrary to the author's moral rights, without the author's consent, is an infringement (s. 28.1).
- A vendor answers for what its AI agent tells a customer: "It should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website. It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot." (*Moffatt v. Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149, para. 27). The customer was awarded "$812.02" (para. 44).
- For infringement of moral rights, "the court may grant to the holder of those rights all remedies by way of injunction, damages, accounts, delivery up and otherwise that are or may be conferred by law for the infringement of a right" (s. 34(2)).

- Same decision, para. 26, duty of care — read — same URL — "Here, given their commercial relationship as a service provider and consumer, I find Air Canada owed Mr. Moffatt a duty of care. Generally, the applicable standard of care requires a company to take reasonable care to ensure their representations are accurate and not misleading."
- Same decision, para. 27, the chatbot isn't a separate entity — read — same URL — "Air Canada argues it cannot be held liable for information provided by one of its agents, servants, or representatives – including a chatbot. It does not explain why it believes that is the case. In effect, Air Canada suggests the chatbot is a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions. This is a remarkable submission. While a chatbot has an interactive component, it is still just a part of Air Canada's website. It should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website. It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot."
- Same decision, para. 28, no reasonable care; the other page doesn't cure it — read — same URL — "I find Air Canada did not take reasonable care to ensure its chatbot was accurate. While Air Canada argues Mr. Moffatt could find the correct information on another part of its website, it does not explain why the webpage titled "Bereavement travel" was inherently more trustworthy than its chatbot. It also does not explain why customers should have to double-check information found in one part of its website on another part of its website."
- Same decision, para. 31, a contractual defence must be proved with the contract — read — same URL — "Air Canada is a sophisticated litigant that should know it is not enough in a legal process to assert that a contract says something without actually providing the contract." and "It did not, so it has not proven a contractual defence."
- Same decision, para. 33, measure of damages — read — same URL — "Mr. Moffatt is entitled to be put in the position they would have been in if the misrepresentation had not been made."
- Same decision, para. 44, order — read — same URL — "I order Air Canada to pay Mr. Moffatt a total of $812.02, broken down as follows: a. $650.88 in damages, b. $36.14 in pre-judgment interest under the Court Order Interest Act, and c. $125 in CRT fees."
- A tribunal decision. It doesn't bind other courts.
