## Clarification

- Software can't be an author. The Copyright Act "requires all eligible work to be authored in the first instance by a human being" (*Thaler v. Perlmutter*, D.C. Cir. 2025).
- Using AI tools "to assist rather than stand in for human creativity does not affect the availability of copyright protection for the output" (US Copyright Office, 2025).
- Applicants "should not list an AI technology or the company that provided it as an author or co-author" (88 Fed. Reg. 16190).
- Providing false author information as copyright management information, knowingly and with intent to induce, enable, facilitate or conceal infringement, is barred by 17 U.S.C. § 1202(a).

- Thaler v. Perlmutter, D.C. Cir., No. 23-5233, decided March 18, 2025: the Copyright Act requires human authorship — read — https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2025/03/23-5233.pdf — "The Creativity Machine cannot be the recognized author of a copyrighted work because the Copyright Act of 1976 requires all eligible work to be authored in the first instance by a human being."
- Same opinion: Copyright Office policy applied — read — same URL — "This policy requires work to be authored in the first instance by a human being to be eligible for copyright registration."
- Same opinion: the work was described as autonomously created by AI (Board decision quoted) — read — same URL — "autonomously created by artificial intelligence without any creative contribution from a human actor[.]"
- Same opinion: scope limit — read — same URL — "we need not address the Copyright Office's argument that the Constitution itself requires human authorship of all copyrighted material."
- Same opinion: authorship requirement applies to work made for hire — read — same URL — "The authorship requirement applies to all copyrightable work, including work-made-for-hire."
- Copyright Registration Guidance: Works Containing Material Generated by Artificial Intelligence, 88 Fed. Reg. 16190 (Mar. 16, 2023): human creation — read — https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/html/2023/03/16/2023-05321.html (document page: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/03/16/2023-05321/copyright-registration-guidance-works-containing-material-generated-by-artificial-intelligence) — "to qualify as a work of `authorship' a work must be created by a human being" (quoting the Compendium).
- Same Guidance: do not list AI as author — read — same URL — "should not list an AI technology or the company that provided it as an author or co-author simply because they used it when creating their work."
- Same report — read — same URL — "Based on the functioning of current generally available technology, prompts do not alone provide sufficient control."
- Same report, date line — read 2026-11-04 — same URL — cover: "january 2025"; acknowledgements signed: "Shira Perlmutter Register of Copyrights and Director U.S. Copyright Office January 29, 2025".

## Tools and authorship: cases and definitions

- *Burrow-Giles Lithographic Co. v. Sarony*, 111 U.S. 53 (1884) — read 2026-11-04 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/111/53 — "These findings, we think, show this photograph to be an original work of art, the product of plaintiff's intellectual invention, of which plaintiff is the author".
- Same opinion, the defendant's argument the Court rejected — read — same URL — "a photograph is the mere mechanical reproduction of the physical features or outlines of some object".
- *Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.*, 499 U.S. 340 (1991) — read 2026-11-04 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/499/340 — "The sine qua non of copyright is originality. To qualify for copyright protection, a work must be original to the author." Also: "the requisite level of creativity is extremely low; even a slight amount will suffice."
- Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2 (U.S. Copyright Office, Jan. 2025) — read 2026-11-04 — https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf — "Questions of copyrightability and AI can be resolved pursuant to existing law, without the need for legislative change."
- Same report — read — same URL — "Midjourney users can set a seed (e.g., "123") and receive nearly identical images when repeating the same prompt."
- 17 U.S.C. § 101, "computer program" — read 2026-11-04 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/101 — "A "computer program" is a set of statements or instructions to be used directly or indirectly in a computer in order to bring about a certain result."
- *Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc.*, 593 U.S. 1 (2021), the Sun Java case — read 2026-11-04 — https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/18-956_d18f.pdf — "the creation of new creative expression (the code independently written by Google)"; "the general copyright protection that Congress provided for computer programs"; "the Java programming language, a language invented by Sun Microsystems (Oracle's predecessor)". On the Federal Circuit: "That court held that both the API's declaring code and its organizational structure could be copyrighted." The Supreme Court itself assumed copyrightability and decided on fair use.
- *Oracle America, Inc. v. Google Inc.*, Fed. Cir. Nos. 2013-1021, -1022, decided May 9, 2014 (750 F.3d 1339) — read 2026-11-04 — https://www.cafc.uscourts.gov/opinions-orders/13-1021.opinion.5-7-2014.1.pdf (same text: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-ca13-13-01021/pdf/USCOURTS-ca13-13-01021-0.pdf) — "For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the declaring code and the structure, sequence, and organization of the 37 Java API packages at issue are entitled to copyright protection." Also: "the district court erred in applying the short phrases doctrine to find the declaring code not copyrightable."

## Liability: copyright management information

- 17 U.S.C. § 1202(a) — read 2026-11-04 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/1202 — "No person shall knowingly and with the intent to induce, enable, facilitate, or conceal infringement— (1) provide copyright management information that is false, or (2) distribute or import for distribution copyright management information that is false."
- § 1202(c)(2), what counts as copyright management information — read — same URL — "The name of, and other identifying information about, the author of a work."
- § 1202(b) — read — same URL — "No person shall, without the authority of the copyright owner or the law— (1) intentionally remove or alter any copyright management information".
- 17 U.S.C. § 1203(c)(3)(B) — read 2026-11-04 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/1203 — "statutory damages for each violation of section 1202 in the sum of not less than $2,500 or more than $25,000."
